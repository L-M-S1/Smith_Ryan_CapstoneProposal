# Instructions

Walk through each inject in order and decide what your company does. Use your own network, people, and plans.

Inject 1 (Monday, 7:40 am): An employee reports they can't open files on the shared drive. The file names now end in ".locked," and there's a text file on their desktop demanding payment.

Inject 2 (8:15 am): Two more departments report the same thing. The help desk finds that a staff member opened an email attachment labeled "Invoice" on Friday afternoon.

Inject 3 (9:30 am): Your backup server is also encrypted. A reporter calls asking whether customer data was stolen.

Inject 4 (Day 2): The attackers post a sample of your customer records online and raise the ransom.

For each inject, answer: Who is notified? What do you contain or isolate on your network? What evidence do you preserve? What do you tell staff, customers, and leadership? Which of your policies applies?

# Response

Inject 1: The employee computer will be removed from the Wi-Fi network. Remind every employee that we do not negotiate with criminals, seek out outside assistance. At this stage only employees will be notified until we can ascertain how the data was used.

Inject 2: Isolate the departments, do not power cycle any of the computers, take photos of the system screens and print them out from a known good segment of the network to preserve evidence. Potentially inform other clinics that they could also by at risk of this malware attack.

Inject 3: The backup is no longer good, at this point, if the outside security firm is unable to assist, we may have to scrub everything and start new with brand new configs. Inform the reporter that it is possible but impossible to say for sure until we know what was accessed. 

Inject 4: At the very least, we now know what they have access to and, debating, we may have to pay them. Send out letters to clientele, potentially answer questions from the press. Tell employees to say nothing about the attack, leave all questions to legal and contracted IT company.
